What happens when you ask people to take your work apart

After building a research program, a paper bank and a dozen pages, we asked five reviewers to attack all of it, and ran three more tests aimed at our own leads. This page is the result, written as a short course: seven ideas that separate a real trading edge from a good story, each one tied to something that actually went wrong here, and three small tools you can play with.

Read this first. The five "experts" were AI agents, each told to take a different expert's point of view and to be adversarial. They read our files; they are not real people with real track records, and some of their numbers are from memory or from delayed data. Where we checked a claim ourselves it says so. Nothing here is investment advice, and nothing has been traded: every position is on paper.

2. Seven ideas, each learned the hard way

IDEA 1

If you look often enough, you will find something

A result is "significant at 1%" if it would happen by luck one time in a hundred. Run a hundred tests on noise and you should expect about one such result, and a good chance of at least one. The one you report is the winner of a contest, not a random draw, so it tends to look better than it is.

Here: the statistician counted more than 150 comparisons on the same two years of data: different windows, thresholds, stocks, return definitions and factor fits. H1's headline p-value of 0.006 was never corrected for that.

Every square is a test of something with no real effect. Orange ones "worked" anyway.

IDEA 2

Rows are not independent observations

Counting rows flatters you when the rows move together. Four stocks that all respond to the same news are close to one stock. Trigger days that fall in the same heat wave are one episode, not five. A test treats each row as new information, so it is too confident.

Here: the 59 trigger days sit in 15 clusters; the 41 earnings reactions come four at a time in about 11 quarters, each with a shared sector shock; and all four stocks are one AI-power trade. The reviewers put our truly independent bets at one or two.
IDEA 3

Ask whether the test could ever say yes

Power is the chance a test detects an effect that is really there. Low power is not just a nuisance: a "pass" from an underpowered test is mostly noise, and a "fail" proves nothing. Before you wait a year for a result, check that the sample you will have could answer the question.

Here: H16's pass rule (mean above zero and at least 12 of 20 positive) is met about 25% of the time by pure coin-flips, which we checked. H15 needs four deals by April and the baseline rate gives about a 3% chance. H9 and H13 are a single auction. Some of our tests cannot reach a verdict at all.

Assumes each event's result varies by about 6.4% (derived from the 41-event study: a 1.0% standard error implies 1.0 × √41), a one-sided test at 5%, and independent events. Because events in a quarter move together, real power is lower still.

IDEA 4

A test you design after seeing the answer is not a test

Pre-registration means writing the rule, the sample and the pass mark down before the outcome exists, somewhere you cannot quietly change. It protects you from yourself, because with enough freedom you will always find a version that works. The timestamp is only as trustworthy as whoever keeps the clock.

Here: H16 was registered the evening Talen rose 7.3%. We disclosed it, and the five-day outcome really was unknown, but the idea, the horizon and the hedge were chosen after looking at the data, and our entries are stamped by our own clock and commits. A reviewer called that chasing the latest event.
IDEA 5

Costs decide whether an edge exists

A strategy's gross return is a fantasy; what you keep is gross minus what it costs to trade. Options are the extreme case: thinly traded contracts have wide gaps between the price you can buy at and the price you can sell at, and that gap is paid on the way in and again on the way out. Averaged trade prices are also not prices you could have got.

Here: H14's in-sample gain was +15% gross. The execution reviewer measured round-trip spreads on the strangle in our captured chains (delayed data, so approximate). Our model assumed about 18% round trip; the measured figures were much worse for three of four names.

Required gross return = (1 + cost) ÷ (1 − cost) − 1, paying the cost on the way in and out. Measured round-trip spreads are from 22 delayed Yahoo snapshots as computed by the reviewer and were not re-run by us; half the round trip is taken as the cost each way. TLN's median put volume was 2 contracts.

IDEA 6

Say why it should work

A pattern with no reason behind it is usually a coincidence. For a trading rule the reason has to run through how the company makes money. A one-day price spike is only news to a firm if the firm is exposed to that day's price.

Here: these generators sell most of their output forward and earn capacity payments set years ahead, so one evening's spike changes little in a year's earnings. The reviewer's list of what moves these stocks instead is long: AI-spending sentiment, deal announcements, Talen's own buyback and capacity sale, rates and nuclear policy. We model almost none of it. That is also why a PJM spike signal can look strong in one sample and vanish in another.
IDEA 7

Build less, test more, and keep the books honest

It feels productive to build pages, charts and infrastructure, and some of it is needed. But an outside reader judges the program on its evidence, so the balance matters. The same goes for the paper bank: a market maker earns a thin margin on a huge number of trades, while a handful of directional pairs on $100,000 mostly measures noise.

Here: the investor-style reviewer estimated, roughly and from commit messages, that about 70% of the human effort went to pages and plumbing and about 15% to tests that could have falsified something. The bank's first position is a single pair, and its sealed record uses our own clock. These are fair hits, and both are fixable.

3. The reviewers, and what each one found

Five independent reads of the same project, each from a different seat. They did not see one another's reports.

The statistician

Multiple testing, standard errors and power. Found: the leads are selected maxima; H16's pass rule passes 25% of the time on noise; several forward tests can never reach a verdict. Suggested merging H9 with H13 and retiring H14 as a trade.

The execution quant

Fills, costs and instrumentation, from a market-making point of view. Found: the option cost model is two to four times too kind for three of four names, borrow on a just-popped stock is ignored, and exit prices need a guard against half-finished trading days.

The PJM practitioner

Whether the economics hold. Found: hedged generators should barely react to one evening's price; H9's "at the cap" wording is wrong for its first event; the biggest drivers of these stocks are missing.

The integrity auditor

Logs, jobs and published numbers. Found: the hash chains verify, but they are not externally anchored; a failed bank step can block the day's ledger commit; some page text is stale.

The skeptical investor

Strategy and opportunity cost. Found: no evidence of edge to date, a trail of stories rather than one plan, and a habit of killing our own leads that is worth keeping. Wants one primary test with a fixed sample and a kill rule.

Three new tests

H1 replayed on 2019 to 2023; a realized-move check for H14; and a placebo on New York and Texas spikes. All three were written down before running, and none supports a trading edge.

4. What we would change

Status on Nov 3, 2026: these are proposals. Nothing on this list has been changed yet.

  • Quick fixes. Correct a typo in an event note; make the bank step unable to block the day's ledger commit; ignore any price from a trading day still in progress; soften the claims above ("tamper-evident", "sealed before"); show the replay result beside H1; stop booking H14 in the bank.
  • Amendments, written before any result. H16: pass rule based on the quarter rather than the single event, a beta-matched hedge and a borrow cost. H9: define the entry from PJM's release time, which we have not confirmed, and merge it with H13.
  • Better evidence. Publish the log's head hash outside the repository; replace the flat cost assumption with measured spreads per stock; count every comparison we have made and show how many "leads" survive a correction; label each hypothesis as post hoc, pre-registered or replicated.
  • Stop and focus. No new pages, no hypotheses registered in reaction to the latest news, and one primary test with a fixed sample size and a kill rule.

Method notes

  • How the exercise ran. Five AI agents, each given a different expert role and told to be adversarial, read the repository read-only and reported independently. We then ran three studies, with the rule written before each run, and checked a few of the reviewers' claims ourselves.
  • What we verified. The binomial figure (about 25% for 12 or more of 20) and the deal-count probability (about 3%); that the first auction cleared at $269.92; the hours the spike signal covers (4:00pm to 8:59pm, five hour-beginning hours); that the bank step runs before the ledger commit; and the typo.
  • What we did not. The commit-count split, the spread table, the PJM practitioner's recollections of how the market works, the time of day PJM posts auction results, and the auditor's list of job failure modes.
  • The placebo. New York and Texas spike days matched to PJM's spike frequency, with 24 to 35 episodes each: effects of −38 bp to +29 bp with standard errors of 34 to 40. Too few episodes to rule out a PJM-sized effect, so it is a weak null, not a confirmation.

Educational research. No real-money trades have been made. Paper positions are not a recommendation to buy or sell anything.
