Hunting for hidden structure in PJM data

Correlation only sees straight lines. These seven tools look for rings, humps, hidden loops, tail-only links, digit patterns and repeating rhythms. Each one is run on real PJM, market or grid data, and reported with its failure modes. Several come back with nothing, and that is part of the point.

Read this first. Research and education, not investment advice. A relationship being detectable is not the same as it being tradable. Where a test finds nothing, the page says so, and the sample sizes and shuffle tests behind every claim are shown.

The sandbox: where correlation goes blind

Draw a shape, add noise, and watch five measures try to see it. Pearson sees only straight lines, Spearman only steady rises or falls. Hoeffding's D, distance correlation and a grid-based information score (a stand-in for MIC) look for any dependence at all.

2. Benford's law

In numbers that span many orders of magnitude, about 30% start with 1 and under 5% start with 9. The law is a test of whether data looks naturally grown or invented, but it only applies to data with a wide spread. Mean absolute deviation from the Benford line (Nigrini): under 0.006 is close, up to 0.012 acceptable, above 0.015 nonconforming.

3. Permutation entropy and the complexity plane

Slide a three-value window along a series and record only the up-down pattern of each window. Pure noise uses all six patterns equally (entropy near 1, complexity near 0). Chaos and rhythm leave a fingerprint: lower entropy and higher complexity. Shaded: the region any series can occupy.

4. Recurrence plots and laminarity

A recurrence plot marks every pair of hours where the market was in nearly the same state. Diagonal streaks mean the system replayed a trajectory; vertical streaks mean it got stuck in one state. Two weeks of Western Hub real-time prices are shown, then the rolling scores for the full history.

5. Random matrix theory: how many real drivers?

Correlate 31 daily return series (power stocks, utilities, rates, oil, gas, equity index) and most of the structure is noise. The Marchenko-Pastur law gives the largest eigenvalue pure noise could produce. Anything beyond it is a genuine collective driver.

6. Tail dependence: do hubs lock together only in extremes?

Correlation measures how hubs move together on an average hour. Tail dependence asks something sharper: given one hub is in its top 1% of prices, how likely is another to be? A Gaussian model with the same correlation gives the benchmark; real links that beat it are dormant until stress arrives.

7. Topological data analysis: is there a hidden loop?

Every day's 24-hour demand curve is a point in 24 dimensions. Persistent homology inflates a ball around each day and tracks which loops and clusters survive. A real hidden cycle (like a seasonal oscillation) would show as a loop that persists far longer than anything a loop-free cloud produces.

9. Hawkes process: do spikes breed spikes?

Borrowed from seismology (aftershocks) and now standard for order flow. In a Hawkes process each event raises the chance of more events for a while. Fitted here to the days with a Western Hub evening up-spike of $150 or more, 2019 to 2026.

10. Extreme value theory: how fat is the tail?

Tails are modelled with a generalized Pareto distribution fitted to all evening hours above the 97th percentile of the spread. The shape parameter tells you how heavy the tail is: above 0.5 the variance is infinite, so averages are dominated by a handful of hours.

11. Power spectra and long memory

A physicist's first move on any signal: decompose it into frequencies. Peaks show the daily and half-daily cycles; the slope shows how much slow drift versus fast jitter; detrended fluctuation analysis gives a memory exponent (0.5 is no memory, above it persistent, below it self-correcting).

12. Transfer entropy: which hub moves first?

An information-theory measure of directed influence: how much does knowing hub X's price move this hour reduce uncertainty about hub Y's move next hour, beyond what Y's own past already says. Each cell is a z-score against 40 shuffles (a score of 3 means well beyond chance); read from the row hub to the column hub.

13. Bet sizing: the Kelly sandbox

The one formula every trading desk argues about. For a repeated bet with a win rate and payoffs, the growth rate of capital peaks at a specific fraction (Kelly) and falls off a cliff beyond twice that. Start from the in-sample numbers of the pre-registered strangle, or move the sliders.
