Learning PJM in public, and testing honestly whether there is an edge

This is a hobby project with two goals. The first is to understand how the PJM power market works well enough to explain any price move. The second is to find out, with data and without fooling ourselves, whether any of that understanding can be turned into a trading edge. Everything uses public data, money stays on paper until there is a track record, and the results below include the ones that did not work.

How we keep ourselves honest

Hypotheses first

Every idea is written down with its test before we run it. Nulls get recorded too.

Forward ledger

A cloud job logs signals and predictions every morning before the market opens, in a hash-chained file. Editing the past breaks the chain.

Simple baselines

Every model has to beat a one-line rule and a plain statistical model. Frozen benchmarks make results comparable over time.

No look-ahead

Forecasts are stored as they were issued. A feature is only allowed if it was known when bids were due.

TabPFN-first, model-agnostic

We expect better TabPFN versions to keep arriving, so new ones drop into the same benchmark automatically. The edge, if any, is in the data and PJM framing, not the model.

Kill criteria

If nothing beats the simple baselines after costs in two separate periods, we change direction. Education is the fallback and already a win.

What we have learned so far

PJM's big price gaps are an evening ramp story. At Western Hub, 97% of the largest real-time versus day-ahead gaps over four months fell between 4pm and 8pm, and about 89% of a typical gap was the system-wide energy price, not local congestion. They are scarcity bets: does the evening ramp tighten reserves or not?
Evidence: 20 largest hours in 30 days, 7 days. Small sample, clear pattern.
TabPFN has not beaten a one-line rule yet. For upward price spikes, a rule that says "the higher the day-ahead price, the likelier a spike" scored 0.83 AUC. TabPFN scored 0.745 with prices and calendar and 0.79 after we added load forecasts, weather and wind and solar, which is real but not enough. Gradient boosting scored about the same as TabPFN, so the limit is the data (about 70 spike events), not the model.
Evidence: 900 held-out ramp hours, 67 upward spikes. Differences under 0.05 are noise.
A lead worth testing: generator stocks fall the day after a spike. After upward spike days, an equal-weight basket of VST, CEG, TLN and NRG underperformed SPY by about 1.1% during the next trading day, with no overnight gap, so a trader could in principle act after the open. A possible reason is political risk: spikes feed affordability and price-cap pressure.
Evidence: 28 episodes, t-stat about -3, in-sample, weaker in 2025 than 2026. Not confirmed. The forward ledger has tracked it since Oct 19.
Cheap out-of-the-money puts do not obviously monetise it. Under assumed volatility and spreads, a roughly 1% average edge is about the size of the cost of a short-dated option (bid-ask spread plus time decay). It turns slightly positive if implied volatility stays flat and negative if it drops five points, so real option quotes will decide.
Evidence: Black-Scholes feasibility check with stated assumptions, not a backtest.
The capacity market is the bigger structural story. The last three auctions cleared at the price cap, the system is about 6.8 GW short of its reliability target, and the cap and floor end after the 2029/30 auction. Generators on earnings calls spent most of their time on it.
Evidence: PJM auction releases and company calls; see the Capacity desk.
The balance sheets are not alike. NRG and FirstEnergy have the thinnest equity cushions (about 47 to 48% of enterprise value), Vistra and Constellation look comfortable, and GE Vernova, EMCOR and Amazon have no credit problem at all, so their risk is valuation.
Evidence: Oct 19 snapshot from public statistics pages; adjusted EBITDA guidance used for four names.

    Where we could be wrong

    • Small samples. Seven spike days in a month and 28 episodes in two years can produce patterns by chance. We ran many comparisons.
    • Free data has holes: PJM's load feed was not usable, price history for options is not free, and one stock-price source is unofficial.
    • Generator stocks move with the AI trade and the market. Our simple factor model may not remove all of that.
    • Costs matter. Options spreads, shorting costs and small account sizes can erase a 1% edge.
